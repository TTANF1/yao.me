Oh, Tristan.
I hope to learn from you how to handle other people's emotions with care, in a way that is gentle and soft, yet feels as natural as water finding its course.

I used to think that as long as I was being real (I originally wanted to say sincere), I could express my thoughts simply and directly. Surely there was no need for so much beating around the bush. Later, I wondered: was I really answering sincerely, or was I just taking the easy way out, cutting off all the other feelings and giving an answer that seemed rational and “correct”?

People are made up of emotions and bonds. I don't always grasp what that truly means. But while watching this show, I saw so many things ruined by emotions, and bonds strengthened by those same emotions. Distance created by our ties to one another, and trust and a subtle “balance” that gradually deepen through those same ties.

Both my own experience and the show's stories have led me to believe that making mistakes isn't really something to fear. What matters is recognizing them early, so you can stop trying to justify them. Admitting a mistake isn't that hard either. This is where sincerity carries real weight. Communication really is a complex and profound art, isn't it?

Once, while rolling out a feature, I ran into a problem like this. Very early on, we had agreed that the system should support uploading and using WebP images, and we had given the ISVs the same requirements for their assets. Then, on the day before the full rollout, when all the ISVs were due to connect to the system as planned, we discovered that the backend upload endpoint didn't support WebP. All those earlier stages had somehow managed to miss this simple issue, almost as if they'd agreed to do so. Before long, the initial conclusion in the group chat was to have our operations colleagues tell the ISVs to change their asset format.

Boom. Furious. I wouldn't have been able to stand it either.

I said in the group that this was my fault: I hadn't covered uploading WebP assets during my own testing, and that was indeed my responsibility in the process. I offered a solution: I would convert the assets the ISVs had already prepared into an image format the existing endpoint supported. Within an hour, I would fix it, test it myself, submit it for testing, and release it.

The surprising thing was that none of the worse consequences I'd imagined actually happened. Every stage moved forward smoothly, and the problem was quickly resolved. The PD's tone softened too.

You see, making mistakes isn't so frightening. Stepping up to take your share of the responsibility and helping the problem get resolved is what matters most in that moment.
