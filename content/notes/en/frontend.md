In my view, because of how much AI has improved, the research that used to take a long time — whether a feature can be built, and how — is now drastically shortened. Sometimes you can go straight from a couple of rounds of back-and-forth into implementation.

So frontend developers can spend more time on user experience. Frontend and interaction aren't separate disciplines; a lot of the details we used to skip to hit a deadline can just get done while we're at it. A smooth, polished app experience might not move your performance review or your bonus much, but for users, good products start from the details.

Sometimes I look at the messed-up page layouts, harsh white screens, jarring transitions, and low-quality choppy animations in some big apps, and it really feels like they've gotten their priorities backwards. For the sake of so-called first-load time, or configurability, even standards can become a constraint.

Also, it highlights the importance of self-testing. For quality assurance of the features you build — nobody can guarantee they'll never make mistakes, not even 100% of the time — but having a robust, sustainable quality maintenance system that supports iteration is essential. By now there should be technical approaches that match real-world testing scenarios that can be implemented and consolidated (chrome devtools mcp 🙌).

And consider admin dashboards: some table requirements include small features the product manager never mentioned — like sorting the list by a column (VIP level). I caught it while self-testing and just added it in.

Does it do anything for your performance review? Will the product manager praise you for it? If you bring up this optimization, would the team think, what's there to brag about?

But now I write it down. And when a user naturally uses it without noticing, and thinks, "huh, today just felt smoother, can't really say what's different" —

That's enough for me.
